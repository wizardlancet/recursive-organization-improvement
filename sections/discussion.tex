\section{Implications for human--agent organizations}
\subsection{Comparing capability and organizational change}
The specification makes an otherwise ambiguous comparison operational. A capability-only intervention changes actor profiles while keeping assignment, visibility, authority, and improvement procedures fixed. A workflow intervention changes those arrangements under a fixed procedure. A procedure intervention changes how candidate arrangements are evaluated or retained. A factorial study can compare these interventions and their interactions under the same task population and outcome criterion. The bottleneck example in \cref{sec:foundations} supplies one elementary prediction; Study 1 shows how evidence acquisition and retention alter the value of organizational search; Study 2 shows how review arrangements generate the judgments that can become evidence.

The distinction matters when AI changes both production volume and the work of supervision. Automation can redistribute monitoring demands \citep{bainbridge}; human and machine learning can become organizationally interdependent \citep{sturm}. In Study 1, the same amount of acquired evidence has different value depending on whether the organization reuses it, discards it, or retains it after the relevant task relationship changes. Evidence retention should therefore be evaluated together with the workflow that generates it.

The supplementary audit model in Appendix~\ref{app:audit} examines the earlier decision to acquire information at all. Its finite-horizon value-of-information controller can acquire little evidence under a strong prior, while Study 1 asks what happens to evidence after it has been bought. Together the models distinguish acquisition, retention, and use. Neither an audit count nor a revision count alone measures an organization's capacity to learn.

\subsection{Independence and interaction order}
Distinct agents can share models, training data, tools, and explanations, producing correlated errors \citep{clemen}. Correlated-vote extensions of Condorcet's jury theorem analyze dependent judgments \citep{ladha1992}; information-cascade theory and social-influence experiments examine how exposure changes the use and diversity of private information \citep{bikhchandani1992,lorenz}. Study 2 separates shared private errors from copying and shows that a two-actor public record can conceal this distinction. Recording initial judgments and reveal events makes the mechanisms distinguishable.

Human-first and agent-first interaction differ through the information available when a person commits an assessment. Private commitment can preserve an additional signal, while early advice can supply knowledge the person lacks. The outcome depends on capability, covariance, reliance, and the aggregation rule. The elementary calculation in Appendix~\ref{app:analytical} shows that apparent gains from exposure can equal a reweighting of private evidence. Human--AI studies of appropriate reliance and cognitive forcing motivate behavioral measurements of responses to advice \citep{lee,bansal,bucinca}; their task and participant measures can be used to estimate how exposure changes reliance.

\subsection{Preserving disagreement and separating observation from authority}

Study 2 identifies information exposure and decision rights as distinct design choices. When reviewers can copy an upstream decision, public agreement combines agreement already present in private judgments with exposure-induced conformity. An organization that escalates only visible disagreements can therefore suppress its own opportunity to correct mistakes. Retaining initial assessments, their sources, and reveal order makes these mechanisms distinguishable and supports comparisons of alternative review arrangements.

A gold-labeled audit measures the performance of a review arrangement. A human override changes its final decisions. Their costs and benefits belong to different parts of the comparison: the audit informs future arrangement choices, while the override affects the current task. This distinction helps a team decide whether it needs better measurement, a different escalation rule, or a different final decision maker.

\subsection{How the two studies connect}
\label{sec:study-connections}
Study 1's workflow reversal and Study 2's copying shift both change the relevance of earlier observations. Their different adaptation patterns show that memory must be assessed together with the process generating those observations. The acquisition-matched control isolates evaluator replacement from a changed information path; the Omit echo ablation checks how a deliberately misspecified model interprets the same acquired record. Study 2 holds observation policies fixed, extending the operational comparison to review arrangements whose evidence is generated internally.

\subsection{Validity and next empirical tests}
\label{sec:validity}

The reference checker and public-data mapping demonstrate how the specification links decisions to evidence, permissions, versions, and costs. A comparative evaluation of the specification could measure model-building time, agreement about missing records, and prediction accuracy when users model the same organizations with alternative notations.

Study 1 uses a finite program catalog, known stratum weights, and retained scores from trials conducted at different knowledge states. A wider catalog and adaptive weighting of historical scores would extend the acquisition-matched comparison. Concept-drift, switching-bandit, and change-point methods provide alternatives to fixed-window retention \citep{drift,switchbandit,bocpd}; acquisition costs can be included in those comparisons \citep{costbandit}.

Study 2's next tests concern when to stop paying for observation, how deployed private judgments can supply feedback, and how production volume changes the value of that feedback. Field measurements would also need to estimate human copying of agent advice and whether recording a private commitment changes subsequent behavior. These measurements would connect the error and copying assumptions to a particular review task.

For Study 1, an organizational test would randomize review-routing changes and evaluation coverage across comparable task streams, retain failed proposals, and measure escaped errors and human attention over a declared horizon. A second stage could randomize whether teams may replace the evaluation program. Such a design would separate the value of broader evidence from the value of the procedure used to discover and retain that evidence policy.

For Study 2, an organizational test would randomize upstream visibility before private commitment across comparable review tasks, while recording actor identity, initial and final judgments, independent outcome labels, and attention costs. Separately varying escalation and override rights would distinguish the value of additional evidence from the effects of final decision authority. A later stage could randomize whether teams may replace the observation protocol, thereby testing procedure revision beyond the operational arrangement choices studied here.

\section{Conclusion}
Recursive organization improvement asks how human--agent teams change their work and learn whether those changes helped. The proposed specification links organizational arrangements to visible evidence, decision rights, retained versions, and outcome comparisons. In Study 1, evidence reuse removes much of repeated assessment's apparent disadvantage, while obsolete evidence delays adaptation. Acquisition-matched controls leave small, uncertain differences between fixed and replaceable evaluation programs across three reversal timings. Study 2 shows how review arrangements determine which judgments become observable and how shared errors and copying change the placement of human judgment under a budget. Lower decision error can still come with a net loss from continued observation. Organizational improvement should therefore be assessed through the evidence acquired and retained; acquisition-matched comparisons can isolate the contribution of revising an evaluator.
