\section{Implications for human--agent organizations}
\subsection{Comparing capability and organizational change}
The specification makes an otherwise ambiguous comparison operational. A capability-only intervention changes actor profiles while keeping assignment, visibility, authority, and improvement procedures fixed. A workflow intervention changes those arrangements under a fixed procedure. A procedure intervention changes how candidate arrangements are evaluated or retained. A factorial study can compare these interventions and their interactions under the same task population and outcome criterion. The bottleneck example in \cref{sec:foundations} supplies one elementary prediction; Study 1 shows how evidence acquisition and retention alter the value of organizational search; Study 2 shows how review arrangements generate the judgments that can become evidence.

The distinction matters when AI changes both production volume and the work of supervision. Automation can redistribute monitoring demands \citep{bainbridge}; human and machine learning can become organizationally interdependent \citep{sturm}. In Study 1, the same amount of acquired evidence has different value depending on whether the organization reuses it, discards it, or retains it after the relevant task relationship changes. The conditional implication is to evaluate evidence retention together with the workflow that generates evidence.

The supplementary audit model in Appendix~\ref{app:audit} examines the earlier decision to acquire information at all. Its finite-horizon value-of-information controller can acquire little evidence under a strong prior, while Study 1 asks what happens to evidence after it has been bought. Together the models distinguish acquisition, retention, and use. Neither an audit count nor a revision count alone measures an organization's capacity to learn.

\subsection{Independence, interaction order, and cross-functional breadth}
Independent agent identities do not establish independent evidence. Shared models, training data, tools, or copied explanations can correlate errors, limiting the value of aggregation \citep{clemen}. Correlated-vote extensions of Condorcet's jury theorem analyze aggregation when votes are dependent \citep{ladha1992}; information-cascade theory explains how observing predecessors can displace private information \citep{bikhchandani1992}. Social influence can also change the diversity of judgments \citep{lorenz}. Study 2 uses an exogenous copying probability rather than an optimizing cascade model; its contribution is the observation-equivalence comparison, the distinction between measurement and override, and the budget-dependent placement of human judgment. An ROI comparison records both pre-existing covariance assumptions and reveal events. It can then vary model diversity, information access, or communication while keeping the decision rule explicit. Section~\ref{sec:actor-review} instantiates this distinction by varying private error dependence and exposure-induced copying separately, while retaining the initial judgments under the blind protocols.

Human-first and agent-first interaction differ through the information available when a person commits an assessment. Private commitment can preserve an additional signal, while early advice can supply knowledge the person lacks. The outcome depends on capability, covariance, reliance, and the aggregation rule. The elementary calculation in Appendix~\ref{app:analytical} shows that apparent gains from exposure can equal a reweighting of private evidence. Human--AI studies of appropriate reliance and cognitive forcing motivate behavioral measurements of responses to advice \citep{lee,bansal,bucinca}; reproducing their effects requires their task and participant conditions, not just matching a curve with selected parameters.

Cross-functional humans can reduce handoffs, interpret errors across domains, and connect agent outputs to downstream requirements. Organization design and coordination requirements identify the dependencies through which these gains could arise \citep{galbraith,cataldo,puranam2021}. The gain must be compared with lost specialist depth, attention limits, and switching costs. A useful experiment varies human breadth while logging transfer count, rework, review delay, and quality at fixed resources. These variables are representable in the specification, while their empirical magnitudes remain task-dependent.

\subsection{Weak-to-strong evaluation and more capable agents}
Weak-to-strong generalization concerns learning under weaker supervision \citep{burns}. In a human--agent organization, a related difficulty appears when reviewers cannot directly assess the outputs or proposed process changes of a more capable agent. Execution capability, evaluation reliability, and authority then become separate design dimensions. The organization can invest in decomposed tests, independent evidence, selective escalation, or stronger evaluation tools; each choice changes the cost and coverage of its improvement procedure. Work on amplified supervision and scalable oversight provides candidate mechanisms for this setting \citep{amplify,oversight}.

Hypothetical artificial superintelligence would widen the gap between execution and human evaluation capability. The same specification would require independently measurable outcomes and explicit authority for revising evaluation procedures.

\subsection{Preserving disagreement and separating observation from authority}

Study 2 identifies information exposure and decision rights as distinct design choices. When reviewers can copy an upstream decision, public agreement combines agreement already present in private judgments with exposure-induced conformity. An organization that escalates only visible disagreements can therefore suppress its own opportunity to correct mistakes. Retaining initial assessments, their sources, and reveal order makes these mechanisms distinguishable and supports comparisons of alternative review arrangements.

Acquiring evidence and assigning final authority also have different consequences. A gold-labeled audit informs the assessment of a workflow. A human review supplies an imperfect judgment, and granting that reviewer an unconditional override can introduce errors into cases that were already decided correctly. Under the modeled independent-arbiter rule, random overrides improve accuracy only when the arbiter's error rate is lower than the conditional error rate of the audited cases. Human involvement should therefore be represented through capability, information access, and authority together.

Finally, identifying a better arrangement does not establish that continued observation pays for itself. The comparison with a fixed blind baseline motivates a separate decision about when further evidence is worth acquiring. This connects review design to the earlier distinction between evidence acquisition, retention, and reassessment.

The two studies connect at three points. Study 1's workflow reversal and Study 2's copying shift before round 25 both change the relevance of earlier observations, although their adaptation costs differ by observation policy. Study 1's trial-matched control holds acquisition and reuse fixed while changing the retained evaluator; Study 2's Omit echo ablation holds acquired records fixed while changing the model used to interpret them. Both comparisons show why evidence volume alone cannot explain learning outcomes. Finally, Study 1's discover-once rule separates discovery from continued search, whereas Study 2's paid policies keep acquiring observations throughout. A learn-then-stop comparator is needed to determine how much of Study 2's acquisition expense can be avoided.

\subsection{Validity and next empirical test}
The specification is evaluated through a reference checker, a public-data mapping, and two controlled simulation studies. This establishes a path from named modeling fields to executable decisions and observable omissions. It does not yet establish lower modeling effort, better inter-rater agreement, or better predictions than alternative modeling notations. Those outcomes require users to model the same organizations under competing specifications.

For Study 1, the catalog, known stratum weights, synthetic error distributions, and cost schedule define the comparison. Program trials evaluate a fixed catalog at a smaller screening budget than operational deployment. Historical program scores average trials conducted at different knowledge states; they are a heuristic selection rule, not a stationary posterior for program quality. Reset discards reusable evidence; cumulative retention assumes past outcomes remain relevant; the fixed window bounds age without detecting a change. These choices explain the limits of the tested learning rules. Concept-drift, switching-bandit, and change-point methods offer principled alternatives for future instances \citep{drift,switchbandit,bocpd}, with acquisition costs represented explicitly \citep{costbandit}.

The acquisition-matched control shows why an improved organizational outcome should not be attributed to the most conspicuous changed component. Periodic program trials also alter when evidence arrives and who can reuse it. The observed gains do not establish that program replacement supplies a stable benefit across the tested change timings. A positive result for open-ended procedure discovery would require a broader candidate space and a comparator with equally informative observations.

Study 2 assumes conditionally independent human errors, one shared-error model for agents, and copying that adds no task information. Its parameters are experimental settings rather than estimates of human or language-model behavior. Paired observation additionally assumes that recording a private commitment does not change the subsequent copying mechanism. The enumerated optimum is specific to the candidate catalog and costs. These assumptions make exposure, dependence, and authority separately testable; their empirical magnitudes require task-specific measurement. Study 2 also holds observation procedures fixed and excludes human reviewers copying agent advice. Deployed private judgments do not enter learning memory without a separate purchase. Learning when to stop, allowing human copying, reusing deployed private judgments as feedback, and varying the observation ceiling and production amortization scale are therefore distinct next tests.

A direct organizational test would randomize upstream visibility before private commitment across comparable review tasks, while recording actor identity, initial and final judgments, independent outcome labels, and attention costs. Separately varying escalation and override rights would distinguish the value of additional evidence from the effects of final decision authority. A later stage could randomize whether teams may replace the observation protocol, thereby testing procedure revision beyond the operational arrangement choices studied here.

\section{Conclusion}
Recursive organization improvement asks how human--agent teams change their work and learn whether those changes helped. The proposed modeling specification connects evidence access, organizational memory, authority, costs, and retention through explicit change contracts. Study 1 shows that evidence has a lifecycle: acquisition and reuse can remove much of repeated assessment's apparent disadvantage, whereas retaining obsolete evidence delays adaptation, and acquisition-matched comparisons narrow the gain attributable to evaluator replacement. Study 2 identifies an earlier part of that lifecycle: review arrangements determine which private judgments become visible evidence. Shared errors, copying, cost, and decision authority jointly shape the preferred placement of human judgment, while identifying a better arrangement need not recover the cost of continued observation. Together, the studies connect how evidence is generated to how it is acquired, retained, and reconsidered. Organizational improvement must therefore be evaluated across that lifecycle, rather than inferred from agent capability or the number of revisions performed.
